PKM Web Notes

I wanted something I could use in class with my students that didn’t involve downloading an app. I really love Obsidian and Joplin but I found that getting those set up with my students (especially the plugins!) caused more grief than I expected. I started fiddling around to see if I could build something rather like Dillinger, but then add some ‘personal knowledge management’ type features and some code-execution ability. I wanted to have it pre-loaded with materials as well (simple markdown notes in a content folder) so that someone else could use it for their use-case. I am also going to use it, I think, as the first layer for a thing I’m calling 'The DH Primer' as well as version 2 of ODATE (although for that one, I think I’ll be using my JupyterLab Desktop Extension which marries note-making to a coding environment).

…‘so what did you do on your summer vacation?’ I may have gotten carried away a bit.

PKM WebNotes is the result.

This document explores the features of the application, not as a simple list of functions, but as a series of affordances to explore the what (the feature itself), the how (its practical application), and most importantly, the why (the cognitive or philosophical purpose it serves).

1. The Interface

The PKM WebNotes interface has three primary zones.

1.1 The Left Sidebar: The Library

  • What: A comprehensive list of all notes within your knowledge base. It supports a hybrid organizational model, displaying both individual notes and collapsible folders.
  • How:
    • Viewing: Click on any note to open it in the main editor. Click a folder’s header to expand or collapse its contents.
    • Organizing: Right-click on a note to Move it to a folder. Right-click in an empty area of the sidebar to create a New Folder.
    • Sorting: Right-click to sort your entire library alphabetically or by modification date.
  • Why: Sometimes we need structure. You can arrange it however you like; happily, you do not need to remember paths in order to use the wikilinks.

1.2 The Main Area: The Workbench

  • What: The central workspace where you engage directly with your ideas. It features a tabbed interface, an editor, and a real-time preview pane.
  • How:
    • Tabbed Interface: Open multiple notes simultaneously for easy cross-referencing. Each tab can be closed individually.
    • Editor Modes: Switch between three views:
      1. Edit: A full-screen, distraction-free Markdown writing environment.
      2. Split: The default view, showing your raw Markdown on the left and a rendered preview on the right.
      3. Preview: A full-screen, read-only view of the rendered note. However this is the view where you can execute your code.
  • Why: The tabbed interface acknowledges that thinking is rarely a single-threaded process. The editor modes cater to the distinct phases of the creative process: the generative chaos of writing (Edit), the structured process of refining and formatting (Split), and the reflective consumption of the finished product (Preview).

1.3 The Right Sidebar: The Serendipity Engine

  • What: A contextual panel that automatically provides metadata and connections related to the currently active note. It includes a word count, a list of Backlinks, and a local Knowledge Graph.
  • Why: This is the application’s intellectual core. While the Left Sidebar is for what you know you’re looking for, the Right Sidebar is for discovering what you didn’t know was connected. It can the process of finding relationships, transforming a static note into a dynamic hub of context and fostering serendipitous discovery. You can export this knowledge graph, and the app will provide you some useful info on clusters of notes, bridging notes, and isolated notes.

2. Core Mechanics

Notes become more useful when they can link together: interesting things emerge from different patterns of connections.

2.1 Markdown: The Lingua Franca of Text

  • What: A lightweight markup language for formatting plain text.
  • How: Use simple syntax like # Heading 1, *italic*, **bold**, and - A list item to structure your documents. The application supports standard Markdown, including tables with “zebra striping” for enhanced readability.
  • Why: Markdown is portable, human-readable, and platform-agnostic. By using it, you ensure your notes are never locked into a proprietary format.

2.2 Wikilinking: Weaving the Web

  • What: The primary method for creating internal links between notes.
  • How:
    • Standard Link: Enclose a note’s title in double square brackets: [[Note Title]].
    • Alias Link: To change the link’s display text, use a forward slash: [[Note Title/a more descriptive link text]].
    • Creation on the Fly: If you link to a note that doesn’t exist (a “broken link”), it is styled differently, acting as a prompt. In the editor, this syntax will appear in your autocompletion list, allowing you to create the note with a single click.
  • Why: The Wikilink is the atomic unit of networked thought. It is a low-friction method for expressing a relationship between two ideas. Aliases provide linguistic flexibility, allowing you to link concepts naturally within the flow of your prose. Broken links are not errors; they are intentional placeholders—a commitment to future thinking and a map of your intellectual curiosity.

2.3 Backlinks: The Unspoken Conversation

  • What: An automatically generated list of all notes that link to the current note.
  • How: Create a Wikilink in Note A that points to Note B. When you view Note B, a link to Note A will appear in the “Backlinks” section of the Right Sidebar, along with the surrounding context.
  • Why: Backlinks answer the crucial question: “In what other contexts have I considered this idea?” They reveal the hidden dialogues between your notes, surface unexpected relationships, and add layers of depth to your understanding. It is a passive, automated system for intellectual discovery.

3: Advanced Capabilities

These are some extras I thought would be useful

3.1 The Knowledge Graph: Visualizing Your Mind

  • What: A dynamic, interactive visualization of the connections between your notes.
  • How: The Right Sidebar displays a “local” graph centered on the active note, showing its immediate neighbors. Click any node in the graph to navigate directly to that note.
  • Why: The visualization is nice, and transforms the abstract network of your ideas into a tangible map. Via the export it allows you to instantly identify clusters of thought, isolated “orphan” notes, and highly connected “hub” concepts, providing a macro-level perspective of your intellectual landscape that is impossible to achieve through lists and folders alone. You could then import this data into more formal network analysis contexts if you wanted.

3.2 Executable Code Blocks: The Computational Notebook

  • What: The ability to run Python code directly within a note and view the output.
  • How: Create a code block using the ```python fence. In the Preview pane, a “Run” button will appear. Clicking it executes the code using an in-browser Pyodide engine, displaying text output, tables, or even plots directly beneath the code.
  • Why: This feature transforms your knowledge base from a passive repository into an active laboratory. It is for the student learning to code, the data analyst prototyping a script, or the researcher running a quick calculation. By integrating computation directly with documentation, it closes the gap between theory and practice, allowing you to “show your work” in a powerful, reproducible way. It uses Pyodide for the kernel. Packages can be installed with micropip, like so:
await micropip.install("networkx")

The Cloud is Someone Else’s Computer

In an era of cloud-based services and data subscriptions, PKM WebNotes is just a simple (more or less) website.

  • What: The application operates on a “local-first” principle. All notes, tabs, and settings are saved exclusively within your web browser’s local storage. (But if you ‘clear the cache’ or delete browsing data, you can lose it).
  • How: It just works. There are no servers, no accounts, and no internet connection required for use.
  • Why: Well, I was trying to go for:
    1. Privacy: Your thoughts never leave your machine.
    2. Performance: The application should be pretty fast, as there are no network latencies. Loading up Python the first time can cause a little pause.
    3. Permanence: Your knowledge base is always available, offline or online, without reliance on a third-party service.

As mentioned, there is Import and Export functionality. You can import existing Markdown files to populate your library, and export your entire knowledge base as JSON, a collection of individual Markdown files, or even export your graph data as CSV for advanced analysis in other tools.

Make It Your Own

The source code and the built app are available from the repo. You could set it up with materials for a course or workshop just by forking it and changing up the content folder. Then to deploy, use Netlify Drop. If you want to poke around in the underlying code, look at the ‘source’ branch. Enjoy!
